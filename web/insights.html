Predicting a 9-category fantasy league: how the model works and what has been tested

Abstract

The league page shows each team's chance to win its matchup. Those chances come from a model that projects every player's stats, estimates who will actually play, and turns the totals into category and matchup chances. Every change to the model follows the same rule: the test is written down before any results exist, the change is built on one past NBA season and judged on seasons it never saw, and it is kept only if it is more accurate on all of them. So far 13 changes have been kept and 17 rejected or not built. The largest gains came from blending in real stats as the season goes on (37% less error), from tracking who actually plays (half the error for injured players), and from a rebuilt weekly engine (about 5% less error). The newest change, adjusting for the opponent and for injured teammates, helps a little (0.2–0.4%) and will matter more as the season goes on.

1. Introduction

In a 9-category head-to-head league, each week two teams compare totals in points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, three-pointers, turnovers (fewer is better), field-goal percentage and free-throw percentage. The team that wins at least 5 of the 9 categories wins the week. To give a chance for that, the model needs three things: how much each player will produce per game, how many games each player will actually play, and how uncertain both of those are.

This page describes each part of the model, the math behind the parts that were built for this league or aren't obvious, and the evidence for every change, including the ones that failed.

2. How changes are evaluated

Each change goes through the same four steps.

  1. Pre-registration. The test, the data and the pass rule are written down before any results are computed, so results can't move the goalposts.
  2. Fitting. Any new numbers the change needs are estimated from one past season (usually 2024-25).
  3. Testing on unseen seasons. The change is judged on seasons that played no part in building it (usually 2025-26 and 2023-24). Past seasons are replayed with random 13-player rosters, day by day, and the model's chances are compared with what really happened.
  4. Decision. The change is kept only if it passes on every test season. A failed test is final.

2.1 Prediction error (the main score)

For matchup chances the score is the Brier score: the average squared gap between the chance given and the result.

\[ \text{error} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (p_i - y_i)^2 \]

\(p_i\) is the chance the model gave in matchup \(i\) (between 0 and 1), \(y_i\) is 1 if that team won and 0 if it lost, and \(N\) is the number of matchups. Saying 0.8 for a team that wins costs \((0.8-1)^2 = 0.04\); saying 0.8 for a team that loses costs \(0.64\). Always saying 0.5 scores 0.25, so anything below 0.25 is better than guessing.

2.2 Calibration

A model can have a low error and still be systematically off, for example always too confident. So predictions are also grouped into ranges (0–10%, 10–20%, …) and, in every range with at least 200 predictions, the average predicted chance must be within 4 percentage points of how often it actually happened.

2.3 Is the improvement real?

Small differences can appear by luck. For the newest tests, the matchups were resampled 2,000 times (a bootstrap): each time, a new set of matchups is drawn at random from the ones played, with repeats allowed, and the difference in error is recomputed. The change passes only if the middle 95% of those 2,000 differences is entirely below zero, meaning it helps in essentially every reshuffle of the data.

3. The model

3.1 Player projections before the season

Projections start from ESPN's and the players' recent history, then get adjusted with ADP (average draft position: where a player typically goes in drafts). Drafters collectively know about injuries and role changes that stat projections miss. Each player's projected value \(v\) is moved toward the value implied by their draft position:

\[ v_{\text{adjusted}} = v_{\text{model}} + \beta\,\big(v_{\text{market}} - v_{\text{model}}\big) \]

\(v_{\text{market}}\) is the value of whoever the model ranks at that player's ADP spot. \(\beta\) is how much to trust the market, estimated from past seasons. The "belief layer" (§4.1) goes further: it lets \(\beta\) differ between seasons, because some years the market is right more often than others, and it treats \(\beta\) as uncertain rather than fixed.

3.2 During the season: real stats take over

Each player's expected per-game stats mix the preseason projection with what they've actually done:

\[ \text{rate} = \frac{K \cdot \text{projection} + n \cdot \text{season average}}{K + n} \]

\(n\) is games played so far and \(K\) is how many games it takes for real stats to get half the weight. \(K\) was estimated separately for each stat from past seasons: 9.5 games for points, 13.6 for rebounds, 24 for steals and blocks (those are noisier, so the model waits longer before believing them).

3.3 Who actually plays

Two pieces. First, each player's share of games played blends their preseason expectation with this season's record, the same way as above, with \(K_{gp} = 34\) team games. Second, for a player who is out, the model uses how long absences actually last. From 3,329 real absences in three seasons, \(S(m)\) is the share that lasted at least \(m\) games (Figure 1).

\[ P(\text{still out at the } j\text{-th next game} \mid \text{out for } m \text{ so far}) = \frac{S(m+j)}{S(m)} \]

For example, \(S(4) = 0.259\) and \(S(5) = 0.218\): a player who has missed 4 games is still out for the next one with chance \(0.218 / 0.259 = 84\%\). Long absences tend to keep going; short ones usually end quickly.

For the next few games, a healthy player who just played is more likely to play again than their season average suggests:

\[ P(\text{plays the } j\text{-th next game}) = \pi + (1-\pi)(1-\rho)^{j} \]

\(\pi\) is the player's usual share of games played and \(\rho = 0.361\) (fit on 2024-25). For the very next game the chance is close to 1 minus a small risk; it drifts back to \(\pi\) further out.

3.4 From players to a matchup chance

Adding up every remaining game gives each team an expected total \(\mu\) and an uncertainty (variance \(\sigma^2\)) in each category. Only 10 players can start per day, so on crowded days the model simulates which 10 start. The chance team A wins a counting category is

\[ p_c = \Phi\!\left( \frac{\mu_A - \mu_B}{\kappa_c \sqrt{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2}} \right) \]

\(\Phi\) turns a gap measured in standard deviations into a probability (a gap of 0 gives 50%, a gap of 1 standard deviation gives 84%). \(\kappa_c = 1.041\) is a small widening factor fit on 2024-25 so the chances aren't overconfident. For FG% and FT% the same idea is applied to each team's makes above league average per attempt.

The nine category chances then become one matchup chance. Categories aren't independent: a big week tends to lift points, threes and assists together. Treating them as independent made the odds too extreme, so the number of categories won is given extra spread \(\psi\):

\[ P(\text{win the week}) = 1 - \Phi\!\left( \frac{4.5 - \sum_c p_c}{\sqrt{(1+\psi)\sum_c p_c(1-p_c)}} \right) \]

\(\sum_c p_c\) is the expected number of categories won and \(\sum_c p_c(1-p_c)\) is how much that number varies if categories were independent. \(\psi = 0.35\) adds 35% more variance, the amount that matched 2024-25. Winning needs 5 of 9, so the cut-off is 4.5.

3.5 Game context (new, Oct 23)

Opponent and pace. Each game is adjusted for the team being played, using what that team has given up this season compared with what the same players usually produce:

\[ \mu_{\text{game}} = \mu \cdot f_o, \qquad f_o = 1 + \frac{n_o}{n_o + K}\left(\frac{A_o}{E_o} - 1\right) \]

\(A_o\) is the total of a stat that players have produced against opponent \(o\) this season, \(E_o\) is what those same players were expected to produce, and \(n_o\) is how many games \(o\) has played. The fraction \(n_o/(n_o+K)\) starts at 0 and grows with games played (Figure 2), so a team that allowed a lot in two games barely counts, while 40 games of evidence counts half. \(K\) is 20 games for assists, steals and turnovers and 40 for the others (fit on 2024-25).

Teammates out. When a teammate who has been playing misses 2 or more games in a row, the remaining players get a bit more of the stats:

\[ \mu_{\text{game}} = \mu \,(1 + \beta_s\, x), \qquad x = \frac{\text{minutes per game of absent teammates}}{240} \]

240 is the total player-minutes in a game (5 players × 48 minutes). \(\beta_s\) was fit on 2024-25 for each stat: 0.082 for points, 0.075 for assists. With a 30-minute player out, \(x = 0.125\), so a teammate's expected points rise by \(0.082 \times 0.125 \approx 1\%\).

6. Limitations

  • The model's 95% ranges for player value contain the real result about 92% of the time, so big surprises happen a little more often than it expects. The one fix tried made things worse.
  • "Day-to-day" injury tags are ignored for now; there is no past data on them yet (§7).
  • The opponent adjustment only applies to the current week, not to future weeks of the season simulation.
  • The value of draft-market information changes from season to season, and one season can't be predicted in advance.

7. Next tests

  1. Day-to-day tags. Every player's ESPN status has been logged since Oct 22. The test runs once about 300 games by day-to-day players are recorded (mid-November).
  2. Early-season expectations. In week 1, players across the league came in about 15% below expectations, mostly from fewer minutes. Past seasons will show whether this happens every year.
  3. Opponents in future weeks, especially the March playoff weeks.
  4. Back-to-backs: whether stars sitting the second night should lower their chance of playing.
  5. Real lineups instead of assuming every manager starts their best players.
  6. End of season: every weekly prediction from 2026-27 gets scored against what happened.
