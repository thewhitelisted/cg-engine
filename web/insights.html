How the odds work, and what we've tested

Last updated Oct 23, 2026

Every chance on the league page comes from a model: a fixed set of rules that turns player stats and the NBA schedule into chances. This page lists every change that has been tried, whether it made the predictions more accurate, and what gets tested next.

How a change gets in

  1. The test is written down first. Before any results exist, we write what will be measured and what counts as a pass. The results can't change the rules after the fact.
  2. It's built using one past season (usually 2024-25).
  3. It's tested on other past seasons it never saw (usually 2025-26 and 2023-24). Those seasons are replayed day by day, and the chances the model gave are compared with what actually happened.
  4. It's kept only if it's more accurate on every test season, not better on one and worse on another. A failed test is final: the same idea isn't re-run until it passes.

How "more accurate" is measured

Prediction error
For each matchup, take the gap between the chance the model gave and what happened (a win counts as 100%, a loss as 0%), square it so big misses count more, and average over every matchup. Smaller is better. Saying 80% for a team that wins gives a small error; saying 80% for a team that loses gives a big one.
Calibration
When the model says 70%, that team should win about 70% of the time. Predictions are grouped into ranges (0–10%, 10–20%, and so on), and in every range the predicted chance has to be within 4 percentage points of how often it really happened.
Test season
A past season that wasn't used to build or tune the change. A model can look great on the data it was built from, so only results on test seasons count.
ADP (average draft position)
Where a player typically gets picked across fantasy drafts. It reflects what thousands of drafters know, including injuries and role changes that stat projections can miss.

What's in the model now

Player projections (built before the season)

KeptDraft-market information

Projections are adjusted toward each player's ADP, so the model doesn't ignore what drafters collectively know.

Player value predictions: error 25% smaller on a test season (7.02 → 5.29).

KeptMarket adjustment only for players people actually draft

Players with no real ADP keep their plain projection.

Error 4% smaller across three seasons (6.99 → 6.68).

KeptDeciding how much to trust the market

Some seasons the draft market is right more often than the projections, some seasons less. The model learns how much weight to give each and allows for that to vary. Three versions were kept, each beating the one before.

Projection error 7% smaller across three seasons (1.564 → 1.457).

KeptLess overconfident weekly odds

The first version was too sure of itself. Spreading the odds out by 18% made them match reality better.

Prediction error 0.2106 → 0.2095 on a test season. Teams given 75–90% now win about as often as predicted.

KeptValuing outcomes by the real prizes

Results are weighed by the actual payouts ($150 / $90 / $60), not just by the chance of winning the title.

Same draft decisions, scored on what actually pays.

During the season

KeptMixing in this season's stats

Each player's expected stats blend their preseason projection with what they've actually done. Real games count for more as the season goes on: for points, real stats get half the weight after about 10 games.

Rest-of-season points predictions: error 37% smaller than projections alone.

KeptWho actually plays

Tracks how often each player really plays. For a player who's out, it uses how long absences actually lasted in 3,329 real cases to estimate when they'll be back. Injured-reserve players return to their team when healthy.

Predictions of games played for injured players: error cut in half (0.148 → 0.074).

This week's odds

KeptDaily lineup limit and short-term availability

Only 10 players can start each day, so on busy nights some games go unused. A player who played last game is very likely to play the next one; a player who has missed several is less likely.

Part of the version below.

KeptCategories that move together

A big week usually shows up in several categories at once (points, threes and assists together). The model accounts for this when it turns nine category chances into one matchup chance.

With the item above: matchup prediction error about 5% smaller on both test seasons (0.130 → 0.123).

New, Oct 23Opponent and pace

Each game is adjusted for the team being played, based on what that team has given up this season. Early on it barely counts; it gets more weight as each team plays more games (half weight after 20 to 40 games, depending on the stat).

Together with the next item: prediction error 0.2–0.4% smaller on both test seasons.

New, Oct 23Teammates out

When a teammate who has been playing misses 2 or more games in a row, the players still on the floor get a bit more of the stats. For example, with a 30-minute player out, a teammate's expected points go up about 1%.

Tried and not kept

RejectedDrafting only to maximise title chance

Did worse on real past seasons.

RejectedWider ranges for surprise seasons

Made typical ranges less accurate while barely helping the extreme ones.

RejectedCorrecting games-played projections

Over-corrected; overall accuracy got worse.

RejectedRecalculating the market weight separately each season

Less accurate overall than one weight from all seasons.

RejectedAn outside projection system (DARKO) as extra input

More accurate for single stats, but less accurate for overall player value in 2 of 3 seasons. It gets re-tested on this season at the end of the year.

RejectedSeparating ESPN's points-league ranking from its category ranking

Looked better at first, then failed when tested forward in time (using only earlier seasons to predict later ones).

RejectedRe-fitting how much each past season and player age matter

No accuracy gain over the simple standard weights.

RejectedPlayers in the same game, and league-wide good or bad nights

When two players face each other, their stats move together slightly, but it made zero measurable difference to matchup predictions.

RejectedEarlier versions of the weekly odds (three attempts)

Either too cautious in the 10–30% range or built with a design error. Replaced by the version kept above.

Not builtLearning other managers' draft habits

Changed title chances by less than half a percentage point.

Not builtSimulating every day of the season

The weekly model already matches real weeks, and the 10-starter limit only costs 0.36% of games.

Checked, no change needed

ConfirmedThe model's built-in assumptions

How fast injured players' games get replaced, how quickly real stats take over, how often teammates miss games together, and games per week were all measured from real data. Every measured value was close enough to what the model already used that no decision would change.

ConfirmedTeammates taking each other's stats

Once you account for who played, teammates' stats don't rise and fall together. Only their games played do (teammates share a schedule), and the model already includes that.

Coming next

  1. Day-to-day injury tags. Right now a "day-to-day" tag doesn't change anything. Every player's ESPN status has been recorded since Oct 22; the test runs in mid-November, once there are about 300 games by day-to-day players to learn from.
  2. Early-season expectations. In the first week, players across the league came in about 15% below expectations, mostly from playing fewer minutes. The plan is to check past seasons to see whether this happens every year, and if so, let real stats take over sooner.
  3. Opponents in future weeks, including the playoffs. The new opponent adjustment only affects this week. Carrying it into the rest of the season, especially the March playoff weeks, needs its own test.
  4. Back-to-backs. Stars often sit the second night of back-to-back games. The model currently treats every game the same.
  5. Real lineups. The model assumes every manager starts their best players. Using the lineups managers actually set would make live odds sharper.
  6. End of the season. Every weekly prediction from this season gets scored against what happened, and the outside projection system gets its re-test.

Known limitation

The model's "95% ranges" for player stats contain the real result about 92% of the time, so surprises happen slightly more often than it expects. The one fix that was tried made things worse, so it's listed here instead.
